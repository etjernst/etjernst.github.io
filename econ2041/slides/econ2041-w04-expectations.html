Discrete, continuous, or categorical?

The recording's rule needs a companion for real data

Essential Concepts, week 3: discrete takes a finite set of values, continuous takes a continuum


But every column in every dataset has finitely many values

  • PISA escs, the socio-economic index: 62 distinct values in 12,136 rows
  • PISA math score: 3,858 distinct values


Ask what the variable measures in the world, not how many values the column has

Three kinds of variables in the world

In the world Example Arithmetic makes sense?
A count number of siblings: 0, 1, 2, ... yes: an average of 1.4 siblings
A measurement math score, socio-economic index, height yes: any value in a range, rounded when recorded
A label degree enrolled in, suburb, gender no: there is no average suburb

Codes look like numbers, and Python will happily average them

Education recorded as 1 = completed primary, 2 = completed high school, 3 = completed a degree


df['education'].mean() returns 2.3


2.3 is a fact about the codes, not about anyone's education

Rounding keeps the arithmetic, binning destroys it

Rounded

  • A score of 61.4 recorded as 61
  • Average of 61, 74, 58: 64.3, and it means something

Binned

  • PISA book: books at home, recorded as 0-10, 11-25, 26-100, 101-200, 201-500, 500+
  • Average of "101-200" and "26-100": nothing


Books at home is a count in the world; the survey turned it into a label at collection time

Two words for the label case, both in use

  • The Essential Concepts recordings say discrete for anything that is not continuous
  • Categorical is the everyday word for the label kind: it names why averaging is nonsense
  • You will meet both; when a variable is a label, say categorical and nobody will object

Where a covariance comes from

Three hundred students, math score against family background

Data: PISA 2022, Australia (OECD), 300 students drawn at random

Step 1: find the two means

Data: PISA 2022, Australia (OECD), 300 students drawn at random

Step 2: each student's two deviations

(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = (+1.09)(+89) = +97

Step 3: the sign of the product is the quadrant

Step 4: add them up: 199 pull up, 101 pull down

The covariance is in nonsense units, so we standardize

\text{cov}(X, Y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = 29 \;\text{index points} \times \text{score points}


r = \frac{\text{cov}(X, Y)}{s_X \, s_Y} = \frac{29}{0.78 \times 91.7} = 0.41

Some take-aways

  • A conditional expectation is a function: one average for every value of X
  • Averaging hides the ends: 14.6 years, inside one country
  • Correlation sees straight lines only, and a sample's r is itself random
  • The covariance is a vote by quadrant; dividing by the SDs removes the units
